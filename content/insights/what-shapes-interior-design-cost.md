"How much will it cost?" is the right first question, and the honest answer is that it depends on a handful of decisions you can see coming. Knowing them makes the first conversation with any designer far more useful.

## Two budgets, not one

Most projects carry two separate costs that are easy to blur together:

- **The design**: the time spent understanding the brief, planning the layout, choosing materials and drawing everything up.
- **The works**: the contractor's labour, the materials, the fittings and the furniture that turn the drawings into a room.

The works are almost always the larger of the two, so the design decisions are what move the total. A good design stage should save you money on the works by getting the plan right before anything is built.

## What moves the number

**Scope.** Refreshing finishes in a sound layout costs a fraction of moving walls, kitchens or bathrooms. Anything that touches plumbing, electrical points or structure adds cost quickly.

**Built-in joinery.** Wardrobes, kitchen cabinets, feature walls and full-height storage are often one of the largest lines in a home interior. Their length, finish and hardware matter more than the size of the room.

**Materials and finishes.** The same wall can be painted, panelled, plastered or clad in stone. The gap between those choices is large, and it compounds across a whole home.

**The condition of what is there.** Older properties often hide problems behind the finishes: uneven floors, tired wiring, leaks. Budget a contingency for the things nobody can see until the work starts.

**Approvals and building rules.** Condominiums usually have renovation rules and working hours set by management, and some changes need approval from the local authority. These affect the programme, and the programme affects cost.

**Time.** A tight deadline means more people on site at once and less room to compare quotes. If you can give the project time, the budget goes further.

## How design fees are usually structured

Design fees are usually set in one of a few ways: a percentage of the construction cost, a fixed fee for a defined scope, or a rate per room or per area. Be careful with per square foot figures you see quoted online: many of them are renovation or package prices that include the works, not the design. Each fee model can be fair. What matters is that you know exactly what is included: how many design options, how many revisions, whether drawings for the contractor are part of it, and whether the designer visits site during the works.

Ask for the scope in writing before you compare fees. A low fee that excludes the drawings your contractor needs is not cheaper in the end.

## Setting a budget a designer can work to

Share a range, even a rough one. It is the single most useful thing you can tell a designer, because it decides which walls are worth moving and which finishes are worth paying for. A designer who knows the budget can make it go further; one who has to guess will either over-design or hold back.
