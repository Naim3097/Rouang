A restaurant interior is judged twice: once by the guest who walks in, and again every night by the team who has to work in it. Most of the decisions that make or break both are made before anyone chooses a colour. These are the ones worth settling first.

## 1. How many seats, and what kind

Start with the number of covers you need to make the business work, then test whether the room can hold them comfortably. Mix matters as much as the total. Tables for two that can be pushed together give you flexibility for families and groups; fixed booths and banquettes give comfort and use wall space well, but cannot be rearranged on a busy Saturday.

Write the target down early. Every later decision, from the width of the aisles to the size of the counter, depends on it.

## 2. The route from kitchen to table

Draw the path a plate takes from the pass to the furthest table, and the path a tray of dirty dishes takes back. Where those routes cross guests queuing, waiting for the toilet or paying at the counter, the service will slow down and people will bump into each other. It is far cheaper to move a wall on paper than to retrain staff around a bad plan.

## 3. Where people wait

Popular restaurants often have a queue. Decide where it goes, whether it is inside or outside, and what people see while they stand there. A good waiting area is part of the experience; a bad one blocks the door and makes seated guests feel crowded.

## 4. What the kitchen needs from the room

Extraction, gas, drainage and cold storage are usually fixed by the kitchen designer and the building. The interior has to work around them, so get the kitchen layout early. Ceiling heights under ducting, the position of grease traps and the space needed for deliveries all affect what the dining room can be.

## 5. Light at lunch and at dinner

A room that feels bright and fresh at noon can feel flat and harsh at eight in the evening. Plan for both. Daylight is generous in Malaysia, so think about glare on window tables at the hottest time of day. After dark, put the light on the tables and the food, keep it out of people's eyes, and use dimming so one room can serve both sittings.

## 6. Materials that survive service

Restaurant finishes are cleaned more often and knocked harder than almost any others. Table tops, chair legs, the base of the counter and the floor near the kitchen door take the most punishment. Choose surfaces for how they look on the first day and for how they look after a year of mopping, spills and chairs dragged across them.

## 7. Sound

Hard surfaces look clean but make a full room loud, and a loud room makes guests leave sooner and talk less. Fabric, timber, acoustic panels and even planting can soften the sound. Decide how lively you want the room to feel before the finishes are fixed, because adding acoustic treatment afterwards rarely looks as good.

## Bringing it together

None of these decisions is about style, yet together they decide whether the style can work. Settle them with your designer at the start and the creative part of the job, the part guests remember, has room to do its work.
